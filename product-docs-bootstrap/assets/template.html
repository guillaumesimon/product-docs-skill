Scope

The product does

  • Quotes, invoices and credit notes compliant with French requirements
  • Email delivery, open tracking, automatic reminders

The product does not do

  • Bookkeeping, VAT returns, tax filings
  • Recurring invoicing (see Roadmap)

Mission

Make getting paid the part of freelancing nobody has to think about.

Why this and not "make invoicing simple". Simple invoicing is a feature and three competitors already have it. What freelancers actually lose sleep over is the money not arriving, which is why automatic reminders and collection by payment link sit at the centre of the product and bookkeeping is explicitly out of scope.

Vision

A freelancer issues an invoice and never touches it again: it gets sent, chased and settled, and the accounting export is ready when their accountant asks for it. Late payment stops being a fact of freelance life and becomes an exception the tool handles.

This is a direction, not a roadmap. What we are actually building next is on the Roadmap, and the one thing we are optimising for right now is on Objective and strategy.

Purpose: let anyone reading a spec ask "who is this for" and get a real answer. A persona is useful when it settles an argument about a design choice; anything that cannot settle an argument is decoration.

Two to four, no more. Built on observed behaviour and the moment that brings someone to the product, not on age and job title. The test: someone in the team can name a real person for each one. If they cannot, it is a fiction and it will be used to justify anything.

Who we build for

P-01

The solo consultant, three years in

Bills four or five regular customers, always the same, on retainer or per day. Invoicing is a monthly chore of twenty minutes that they resent, and one customer always pays a month late.

What brings them
A late payment that hurt, or an accountant asking for documents they cannot find.
What they do today
A spreadsheet or a Word template, sent by hand, chased by an awkward email they put off writing.
What they need from us
That the chasing happens without them, and that they never have to have the awkward conversation.
What loses them
Anything that smells like accounting software. They left a suite because it asked them to pick a ledger account.
Evidence
The dominant profile in the interview set; the one who names late payment unprompted.
P-02

The freelancer in year one

Just registered, has one or two customers, and is mostly afraid of doing something illegal. Every field on an invoice is a question they do not know the answer to.

What brings them
The first invoice they have to issue, usually the same week they get the first customer.
What they do today
A free generator found on Google, or a template a friend sent them.
What they need from us
To be told the document is correct, and not to be asked anything they cannot answer.
What loses them
An empty state that assumes they already have customers, a price before they have sent anything.
Evidence
The drop-off at the first customer step in the onboarding funnel, and the eleven support conversations behind S-031.

How we make money

A monthly subscription, paid by the freelancer, with a free tier that lets someone issue their first invoices without a card. Nothing is charged to the end customer who receives the invoice.

Why a subscription and not a fee per invoice. A fee per invoice would tax the behaviour we are trying to cause. Our users invoice a handful of times a month and would count every send; a flat price makes automation feel free once it is on. It also keeps us out of the position of earning more when the user works more, which several interviewees said they would resent.

The moment money changes hands

The free tier exists to get someone to a sent invoice, because that is the point where the product has proved something. The paywall sits on automation - reminders, payment links, the export - not on the invoice itself. Somebody who only ever needs to send four invoices a year should be able to do that for free and tell a friend.

Where exactly the line falls, and what happens when a limit is reached, is in Plans and permissions.

What makes an account grow

  • Getting paid faster. A user who sees the median time to payment drop renews without thinking about it. This is the retention engine and it is why collection matters more than it looks.
  • The accountant. Once the monthly export is part of a user's routine, leaving means renegotiating with their accountant. It is the stickiest thing in the product and it is not a feature anyone asks for.

Coarse, and deliberately so: email delivery, PDF generation and the payment provider's fee, none of which scale with anything except invoices sent. A user who never sends costs almost nothing, which is what makes the free tier affordable. The actual numbers live in the billing dashboard, not here.

The category we are in

Invoicing tools for individuals and very small businesses in France - the slice between a free PDF generator and a full accounting suite. Buyers arrive through search, usually the week they have to issue something, and they choose in under an hour.

What is moving. Mandatory electronic invoicing is arriving and will force every tool in this category to handle a structured format. It turns compliance from a differentiator into a ticket to play, and it is why that item sits on the Roadmap despite having no metric attached.

The one objective

Retention Keep Pro subscribers past their third month.

Measured by Pro retention at 90 days, by monthly cohort. The exact definition is on Metrics and tracking and is written there only.

Why this one. Acquisition is not the constraint: signups grow on search alone and we have never spent on it. The leak is later - a meaningful share of Pro subscribers stop in the first quarter, and each one was expensive to convince. Fixing the leak makes every other lever worth pulling; pulling them first just fills a bucket with a hole in it.

How we think we get there

Three bets. Each one says what would prove it wrong, because a bet nobody can lose is a slogan.

People stay when the money arrives faster, not when there are more features

The subscription is renewed by someone who can feel the product working. The feeling is a customer paying earlier than they used to - nothing else in the product produces it. So the work goes into the path from issuing to settlement rather than into breadth.

Proved wrong if: cohorts whose median time to payment improved churn at the same rate as the others. On the roadmap: S-027.

The third month is when the habit either forms or does not

Churn clusters right after the second monthly cycle: enough time to have invoiced twice, not enough for the export to have gone to an accountant. We would rather make that cycle land than spread effort evenly across the lifetime.

Proved wrong if: the churn curve turns out to be flat and the cluster was a cohort artefact. Where to look: the retention dashboard cited on Metrics and tracking.

Recurring invoicing is the retention feature disguised as a convenience

P-01 duplicates the same invoice every month. Every duplication is a chance to not bother, and a user who does not invoice for a month is most of the way to gone. Removing the monthly decision removes the monthly chance to leave.

Proved wrong if: the users who duplicate most are already the ones who retain best, which would make this a comfort feature. On the roadmap: S-033.

What we are deliberately not doing

  • Paid acquisition, for as long as the leak is open.
  • Anything that only serves an audience listed under "not our user" on Personas.
  • Breadth for its own sake - a feature whose case is "a competitor has it" needs a retention argument or it does not get ranked.

Purpose: tell the three to six journeys that carry the value: where the user starts, what they are after, where it can get stuck. The screen-by-screen detail lives in the end-to-end test, which is executable and therefore always right.

First invoice

Intent. A new signup should have sent a real invoice in under five minutes, knowing nothing about legal mentions.

  1. They enter their company number. The product derives the legal name and the VAT regime, which removes the tax questions.
  2. They create the customer and the lines on the same screen. No customer record to fill in beforehand.
  3. They check the preview, then issue. This is when the invoice becomes final (see lifecycle).
  4. They email the customer from the product.

Where it gets stuck. Company number not found: manual entry is offered. Invalid customer email: sending is blocked but the invoice stays issued.

Details live in e2e/first-invoice.spec.ts.

Purpose: explain the few things you cannot work out by looking at the interface or by reading a single file. Three to eight mechanisms, one screen each.

Invoice lifecycle

In short. An invoice is free-form while it is a draft, then frozen once issued. Everything that happens next (sending, lateness, payment, cancellation) changes its state without ever changing its content.

FromToWhat causes the move
DraftIssuedThe user issues it. The number is assigned at that instant.
IssuedSentThe email goes out, or the user declares having delivered it another way.
SentOverdueA daily job notices the due date has passed.
Sent, OverduePaidThe payment provider reports it, or manual entry.
Any issued invoiceCancelledA full credit note is created.

Good to know. The customer's details are copied into the invoice when it is issued. Editing the customer record later does not change past invoices.

Where to enter the code. src/domain/invoice/state.ts holds every allowed transition; nothing is decided elsewhere.

Automatic reminders

In short. After the due date, the product sends the customer a series of emails that are increasingly spaced out and increasingly firm, and stops on its own as soon as there is no reason left to chase.

How it unfolds

  1. Every working morning, a job looks for overdue invoices whose next reminder is due.
  2. For each one, it picks the email template that matches the reminder's rank.
  3. It records the reminder before sending the email, which makes the job safe to rerun without duplicates.

Special cases

  • A payment, even partial, pauses the sequence until the user decides.
  • An invoice marked as disputed is never chased.
  • Nothing goes out at weekends: it moves to the next working day.

Why this way. Users do not dare chase customers themselves. The sequence is deliberately not configurable for now (see Decisions).

Where to enter the code. src/jobs/reminders.ts for orchestration; the delays are constants at the top of the file.

Collection by payment link

In short. Bordereau never touches the money. The link opens a page hosted by the provider, the money goes straight to the user's account, and the product learns the outcome from a message sent by the provider.

Good to know. That message can arrive twice or out of order: processing is designed to give the same result in every case. Until it arrives, the invoice stays "Sent", even if the customer has paid.

Where to enter the code. src/integrations/payments/webhook.ts.

Purpose: explain the logic of the split, not the grid. Exact prices and quotas live in the configuration and on the pricing page: copying them here guarantees they will drift apart.

Plan logic

The free plan lets you invoice occasionally and stay compliant. The Pro plan unlocks everything that saves time or brings money in faster: automation, branding, online payment.

Non-negotiable principle. Access to a legal document that has already been issued is never removed, whatever the plan or the subscription state.

What happens at the limit

Free quota reached: the user can still prepare drafts, but issuing is blocked with an invitation to upgrade. Moving from Pro back to Free: automations stop, nothing is deleted.

Role logic

Two roles only. The owner does everything. The invited accountant reads and exports, but creates nothing and cannot touch the subscription. There is no role in between, by choice: the target is a freelancer working alone.

📩 Your customers, without the typing

  • ✨ Forward any email from a customer to your private Bordereau address. The record is waiting for you a minute later.
  • 👀 Nothing is saved until you say so: every field is shown next to the line it came from.

Pro accounts, gradual rollout.

Behaviour

  1. Each account gets a private forwarding address, shown on the empty customer list and in the new-invoice screen.
  2. The user forwards any email from their customer to that address.
  3. Within a minute, a proposed customer record appears in the product, with the source email quoted next to each field.
  4. The user reviews, corrects and confirms. Nothing is saved before that confirmation.

Edge cases

  • Nothing usable in the email: the user is told so and offered the manual form, prefilled with the sender's address.
  • The customer already exists: the product proposes updating the existing record instead of creating a duplicate.
  • The model is unavailable: the forwarded email waits in a queue, and the manual form stays available.

Acceptance criteria

  • A forwarded email with a standard signature yields a correct name and address without manual edits.
  • No customer record is ever created without an explicit confirmation.
  • The forwarded email is deleted once processed, within a day at most.
  • The feature is fully hidden when its flag is off.

Out of scope

  • Reading attachments (purchase orders, PDFs)
  • Creating the invoice itself from the email
  • Connecting a mailbox

Concepts and how they relate

An account represents a freelancer and their business. It owns customers and invoices. An invoice targets one customer, contains lines, and can receive several partial payments. A credit note always attaches to an issued invoice and cancels all or part of it. Reminders are the history of emails sent for an overdue invoice.

Choices to know before touching the schema

  • An issued invoice is a snapshot. It embeds a copy of the customer's and the seller's details. Never rebuild a PDF from the current records.
  • Drafts and invoices share one table. The number stays empty while the status is draft.
  • Amounts are whole cents. No decimals in the database; rounding happens once, at line level.
  • Nothing issued is physically deleted. Deleting an account anonymises it.
  • Isolation between accounts is enforced by the database, not by application code.

  • Types used by the code: src/domain/types.ts

Six principles

  1. Explain, do not copy. If a piece of information can be obtained with a database query or by opening a file, the docs give the path, not the content.
  2. The docs describe the present. History goes in Changelog and Decisions, the future in Roadmap and Specs.
  3. One piece of information, one place. Everywhere else, a link.
  4. Every page has a trigger. We know which kind of change forces us to reopen it.
  5. Same pull request as the code. A behaviour change without a docs update is not done.
  6. Every explanation ends with a door into the code. A file, a test, a dashboard.

## Product documentation

The product documentation lives in docs/product/index.html.
Its goal is understanding: what the product does, how its mechanisms work, why.
Details are then explored in the code.
Read it before changing an area you do not know.

Golden rule: explain, do not copy.
Never write in the docs what can be read in the database, the configuration or
the code: columns, event lists, prices, quotas, flag states, metric values,
endpoints, environment variables. Explain the principle, then cite the file.

Before closing a task, ask one question: would someone who read the docs
yesterday misunderstand the product today because of my change?
- No: do not touch the docs.
- Yes: open "Maintaining these docs", table "Which change updates which pages",
  and fix the affected pages in the same commit.

On every page you touch: data-verified = today's date, data-status = "ok".
If you spot a gap you cannot resolve: data-status = "check" and describe the gap
at the top of the page.
If the change is visible to the user, add a Changelog entry - from the spec's
future changelog when there is one.

Specs: one page per project, a section with data-parent="specs" copied from an
existing spec. Set data-spec-id, data-spec-status, data-target, data-updated.
The index on the Specs page builds itself. Every spec carries a "Future changelog"
block, div.release: the announcement users will read when it ships, a title and
two or three lines, emoji, benefits not features. Write it before the build; if
it cannot be written, the benefit is not settled and the spec is not ready.
When a spec ships, move its lasting content to the living pages, copy the future
changelog into the Changelog entry, set data-spec-status="shipped" and stop
editing it.

Handover: every spec lists the pages it will have to feed, one

  •  per target inside a 
      . When your change
makes a spec's behaviour real - a flag removed, a gate deleted, a job switched
on - move each line's content into its target page, in that page's own shape and
in the present tense, then tick the line with data-done="". A spec is not
shipped until every line is ticked. Open lines on a shipped spec are listed as
handover debt on the home page, under the project's name.

Business card: five pages - Pitch mission and vision, Personas, Business model,
Market and competitors, Objective and strategy. Nothing on them is in the code,
so the golden rule inverts: the danger is invention, not copying. Never edit them
without an explicit yes from the user, and never on an ordinary commit.
Ask the second question only when something was actually decided: did this work
settle who the product is for, how it makes money, what it is optimising for, or
where it stands against a competitor? Trigger list, closed:
- a new way of charging, or the paywall moving -> Business model
- a group of users served on purpose for the first time, or dropped -> Personas
- the metric the team optimises for changing, or a bet settled -> Objective and
  strategy, then re-read the Roadmap
- the product's promise changing: new surface, new category, pivot -> Pitch
- a competitor moving, or a new one starting to cost deals -> Market
Not triggers: a new feature, a price or quota moving, a metric's value moving, a
competitor shipping something, a strategy conversation that did not conclude, one
user asking for something a persona does not cover.
When one fires: say what you noticed and which page it changes, wait for a yes,
edit one paragraph, move data-verified and the person-and-date in data-sources,
and record a Decision if the change is structuring. If the answer is no, drop it
and do not raise it again for the same change.

Objective: exactly one, on the strategy page's data-objective - acquisition,
activation, retention or revenue. Every spec carries a line saying how it serves
it, or why it is the exception. Replacing the objective is a Decision; adding a
second is not an option.

Roadmap: one row per live project, in priority order, in the tbody[data-roadmap].
Point it at its spec with data-spec and set data-horizon. Rank, project name and
spec status are filled in from the spec page, so never type them in the row.
Reordering rows is how priority is changed; a shipped or dropped project loses
its row.

Writing rules:
- One piece of information, one place. Elsewhere, a link.
- A mechanism is explained as: in short, how it unfolds, special cases, why,
  where to enter the code. One screen at most.
- A business rule always has: identifier, intent, why, file, test.
- Never reuse an identifier (BR, D, S, RB). A retired entry stays, marked as such.
- Use the Glossary terms, no synonyms.
- Everything is written in English.

    • Periodic review

    Once a week, an agent rereads each page against the code it cites and asks whether the explanation is still true. It fixes what it can in a pull request and sets the rest to "Needs review". The register on the home page serves as the dashboard.
